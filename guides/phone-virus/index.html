Phone Malware Removal Guide· Version 1.0 · November 2026⬇ PDF
Read this first. If you suspect a partner, ex-partner or someone close to you installed monitoring software (stalkerware) on your phone, do not remove it yet. Removal can alert that person and destroy evidence. Contact a support service and plan your next steps from a different device. See the Coalition Against Stalkerware at stopstalkerware.org.
How to use this guide. Work through the steps in order and stop as soon as the problem is resolved. Most infections are solved in Step 1.
  1. Find and remove the malicious app
  2. Scan the phone and secure your accounts
  3. Factory reset, the correct way
  4. Reinstall the operating system
  5. Maximum protection for high-risk users

1Find and remove the malicious app

About five minutes. Most phone infections come from an app the owner installed.

Android

  • Restart in Safe Mode. This starts the phone with every app you installed disabled. If the pop-ups or unusual behavior stop, one of your apps is responsible.
  • Uninstall your most recently installed apps one at a time until the problem is gone.
  • If an app cannot be uninstalled, it has probably given itself administrator rights. Open Settings → Security → Device admin apps, revoke its access, then uninstall it.
  • Open Settings → Accessibility and disable any service you do not recognize. This permission lets an app read your screen and act on your behalf.

iPhone

  • Remove calendar subscriptions you did not add. These are the usual source of fake "virus" alerts.
  • Open Settings → General → VPN & Device Management and remove any profile you did not install.

All phones

  • In your browser, block notifications from any website you do not recognize.

If the problem continues, go to Step 2.

2Scan the phone and secure your accounts

No single scanner catches everything, and sometimes the phone is clean but an account is compromised.

  • Android: run a Google Play Protect scan, then a second scan with Malwarebytes (free).
  • iPhone: avoid paid "antivirus" apps. iOS does not allow apps to inspect other apps, so they cannot scan the device.
  • Suspected commercial spyware: use iVerify Basic (iPhone and Android) or Amnesty International's free Mobile Verification Toolkit.
  • Accounts: review signed-in devices for your Google account and Apple Account, and WhatsApp (Settings → Linked devices). Sign out anything you do not recognize.
  • Change your passwords from a computer you trust, not from the affected phone.

If the infection persists, go to Step 3.

3Factory reset, the correct way

  • Back up photos, contacts and files. Do not back up apps: restoring an app backup can reinstall the malware.
  • Reset the phone and set it up as a new device.
  • Install all system updates before anything else, then download your apps again from the official store.

A factory reset erases user data but not the system partition. Malware that installed itself there can survive.

If the problem returns after a reset, go to Step 4.

4Reinstall the operating system

  • Google Pixel: use the Android Flash Tool in Chrome. It erases the device and requires an unlocked bootloader; lock it again when finished.
  • Samsung: use Smart Switch emergency recovery, or visit an authorized service center.
  • iPhone: put the phone in recovery mode, connect it to a computer and choose Restore. This installs a fresh, Apple-signed copy of iOS.
  • Unbranded devices with no official firmware cannot be reliably cleaned. Replace them.

5Maximum protection for high-risk users

For journalists, activists and anyone who may be targeted by commercial spyware.

  • Restart your phone at least once a week, as the NSA recommends. Many spyware infections do not survive a reboot.
  • iPhone: enable Lockdown Mode. A genuine Apple threat notification never asks you to click a link or install anything.
  • Android 16: enable Advanced Protection.
  • Free expert help: Amnesty International Security Lab and the Access Now Digital Security Helpline.

Prevention

  • Keep the phone updated. Security updates alone would have blocked the 2026 Play Store campaign discussed in the video. A phone that no longer receives updates should be replaced.
  • When buying an Android device, confirm it is Play Protect certified. Avoid unusually cheap no-name devices (see the FBI's BadBox 2.0 warning).
  • Pop-ups claiming your phone "has viruses" are advertisements. The cleaner apps they promote are a common source of infection.
